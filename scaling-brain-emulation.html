Thesis summary · The Mdrn Urban

Copying a brain stopped being science fiction. It became a budget.

Isaak Freeman spent two years in MIT's Boyden Lab interviewing roughly fifty researchers and synthesising hundreds of papers to answer one question: what would it actually take to emulate a brain, end to end? His answer is not a prophecy. It is a survey of three fields that rarely speak to each other — mapping wiring, recording activity, and simulating both — translated into the same units so you can finally see which constraint is binding.

“These milestones usher in a new era where mapping, simulating, and emulating brain neural circuits become engineering challenges, not speculative research.” Isaak Freeman

The question is no longer whether a brain can be mapped and simulated, but at what scale, resolution and cost.

01An alternative path to AI

A mind that is human by construction.

The thesis opens on an uncomfortable asymmetry: machine learning now matches or beats people across chess, mathematics, code, language and protein structure, while neuroscience inches along. Freeman's framing is that emulation offers a different route to advanced intelligence — one that is human by construction rather than trained to imitate humans, and so inherits our motivations and cognitive architecture instead of approximating them from text. The rest of the case is less exotic and more immediately useful: digital brains as testbeds for drugs and stimulation, experiments that dodge the skull and the immune system, empirical traction on consciousness, motor priors for robots, and a species-level backup.

02Connectomics

From 302 neurons to 139,255.

In 1986 researchers finished the wiring diagram of a roundworm: 302 neurons and some 7,500 synapses. For decades that stood alone. Then in October 2024 a team of over 200 scientists published the complete adult fruit fly brain — 139,255 neurons joined by 54.5 million synapses, the largest complete brain ever mapped. The cost curve moved with it: reconstruction and proofreading fell from roughly $16,500 per neuron to about $100 in zebrafish larvae. The catch is who does the work. Proofreading the fly alone consumed more than 33 person-years, and human verification still swallows over ninety per cent of a connectomics budget.

03Functional recording

Wiring is not enough. You need the traffic.

A connectome is a road map with no cars on it. To know what a circuit does you have to watch it run, and the number of neurons that can be recorded at once has been doubling roughly every 7.4 years — recently faster. The larval zebrafish is the showpiece: optically transparent, vertebrate, about 100,000 neurons, and now imaged across nearly the whole brain, with one dataset capturing over 70,000 neurons at around one hertz. Mammals are where it stalls. Light does not travel through a mouse brain, so whole-brain coverage at single-neuron, single-spike resolution stays out of reach, and molecular detail — which receptors, which ion channels — is missing almost everywhere.

04The surprising part

Raw compute is not the wall.

This is the section that reframes the whole problem. Freeman brackets the compute bill with two back-of-envelope estimates. Take the simplest useful neuron model and 86 billion of them in real time, and you need roughly 3.4 petaFLOP/s — about one modern data-centre GPU. Take the most biologically detailed model instead, with thousands of compartments and synapses each, and you need on the order of 1e20 FLOP/s — roughly a hundred-thousand-GPU cluster of the kind already being built for AI. Both ends are affordable today. The real squeeze is elsewhere: over thirty years processing power improved about threefold every two years while memory bandwidth managed 1.6× and interconnect 1.4×. That gap is the memory wall, and it is what actually limits brain-scale simulation.

“Human-scale brain simulations are surprisingly computationally accessible today in terms of raw computational power.”

Isaak Freeman
05Benchmarks

Nobody agrees how to tell if it worked.

Suppose you build the thing. How would you know it is any good? The thesis treats this as the field's quiet emergency. You cannot simply check whether the simulation fires the same spikes as the animal, because real brains are stochastic — run the same fish twice and you get different traces from the same circuit. So success has to be defined against distributions rather than exact sequences, and against behaviour rather than raw activity, at specific scales and specific observables. Freeman's argument is the one that made AI legible: the field needs shared benchmark suites before it can tell progress from storytelling.

06The mega-project

Somewhere between a genome and a bomb.

Freeman prices the effort by analogy. The Human Genome Project ran about $5B over thirteen years; the Manhattan Project exceeded $30B in three; Apollo cost roughly $257B. His range, drawn from his estimates and interviews, is $5–50B over 10–25 years — more than a genome, less than a Moon landing. Machine learning is the biggest lever on that number: one adaptive electron-microscopy method already cut acquisition time sevenfold, and networks constrained by the fly connectome have predicted activity across 64 neuron types from wiring alone. Two unglamorous problems remain. A single cubic millimetre of tissue imaged at electron resolution produces about a petabyte, so a whole human brain runs to exabytes. And sample preparation — slicing, staining, embedding — is still artisanal handwork that needs the automation of a chip fab.

One idea to keep

Start with the worm. Every organism is a proving ground for the next.

The honest conclusion is that no pillar is close to human scale, and the path is not one moonshot but a chain of smaller ones. The worm, the larval zebrafish and the fruit fly are where structure, function and molecular data can all be had at once — so they are where the methods get built and tested. It is a useful habit far outside neuroscience: when a problem looks impossibly large, find the smallest version of it where every measurement you need is actually obtainable, and earn the right to the next size up.
