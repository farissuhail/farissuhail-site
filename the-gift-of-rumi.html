The world’s favourite Muslim poet, read without his Islam.

Emily Jane O’Dell has studied Sufism in two registers at once: academically, through teaching and research at Harvard, Columbia and the American University of Beirut, and in practice, learning from a Mevlevi master and his dervishes in Istanbul. Her argument is that the Rumi most English readers know has been separated from the tradition that produced him, and that the separation is not a small editorial matter.

Rumi was a Muslim jurist and preacher whose verses are saturated with Qur’an and hadith. Most English versions quietly removed that, and called the result universal.

01The Rumi problem

Enormously loved, and largely unread.

Rumi has been among the best-selling poets in the English-speaking world for decades, quoted on walls, at weddings and across social media. Almost none of that circulation touches what he actually wrote. The lines people share are usually reworkings at several removes from the Persian, selected for their compatibility with a modern spiritual register that has no particular religion in it. O’Dell’s starting point is simple and hard to argue with: a thirteenth-century Muslim scholar has been made to sound like a contemporary writer on love, and almost nobody reading him in English has been told.

02Who he actually was

A jurist and a preacher before he was a poet.

Jalal al-Din Muhammad, born in the Persianate east in the early thirteenth century and settled in Konya in Anatolia, was a teacher of Islamic law and a preacher with a following before any of the famous poetry existed. That biography is not background colour; it is the grammar of the work. The Mathnawi is built on Qur’anic narrative and prophetic tradition the way a commentary is built on its text, and readers who know the source hear it constantly. Remove it and the verses still sound beautiful — they simply stop meaning what they said, the way a sermon quoted without its scripture becomes a collection of striking sentences.

03What she restores

Translated from the Persian, with the references left in.

The concrete contribution of the book is a body of fresh translations made from the original language rather than reworked from earlier English, presented with the religious and mystical context restored around them. Practically, that means the reader is told when a line is glossing a verse of the Qur’an, when an image belongs to an established Sufi vocabulary rather than to general poetry, and when a word carries a technical sense in the tradition. It is a slower read than the anthology versions, and the difference is the same as between a translated proverb and a translated proverb with its culture attached.

04The forty days

She wrote it from inside the lodge.

Interleaved with the poetry is a first-person account: a forty-day retreat in Istanbul, learning the turn — the Mevlevi practice most outsiders know only as whirling — under the master of a living lodge. The memoir passages are what make the book readable rather than merely corrective, and they carry an argument of their own. A tradition of this kind was never designed to be received through a book. It was transmitted through a teacher, a discipline and a community over time, and a reader picking up couplets online is receiving the output of a system while skipping the entire system.

A line lifted out of a discipline is not a shortcut through the discipline. It is a souvenir.

05Read it with your own grounding

A memoir and a translation, not a work of fiqh.

Two things should be said plainly, and the book says neither loudly. First, tasawwuf and specifically the practices of particular orders have been debated within Muslim scholarship for centuries, and Muslim readers will approach this material from positions that differ, sometimes sharply. Nothing in a summary settles that, and this one does not try. Second, O’Dell writes as a scholar and a practitioner-participant rather than as a jurist: the book is strong on context, language and lived experience, and it is not the place to look for rulings. Read it for what it is genuinely excellent at — restoring the Islamic frame to a poet who has been read outside it for fifty years — and take questions of practice to the scholarship you already trust.

06What you take away

A habit of asking who translated this, and from what.

Even if you never open the Mathnawi, the transferable lesson is about transmission. Every text that reaches you across a language and eight centuries has passed through hands that made choices — what to include, what to soften, what a modern reader would find difficult, what would sell. The useful reflex is to ask of any translated wisdom: who made this version, from which source, for which audience, and what did they decide you did not need. That question protects a reader far beyond Rumi.

One idea to keep

Context is not decoration. It is the meaning.

A verse can survive translation and still lose everything, if what it was answering has been left behind. That is the case this book makes, and it applies well past poetry — to every quotation that arrives on a screen without its source, its occasion, or the tradition that gave it its weight.
