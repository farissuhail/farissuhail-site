Book summary · The Mdrn Urban

A war story that refuses to enjoy the war.

Almost everything people remember about Troy is not in the Iliad. No wooden horse, no burning city, no Achilles heel. The poem takes a ten-year siege and looks at roughly seven weeks of it, in the tenth year, and announces its real subject in the opening line: the rage of one man, and what that rage costs the people standing nearest to him.

It is not a poem about winning a war. It is a poem about what men destroy while defending their pride, and the one night two enemies put it down.

01What it actually covers

A very small window on a very long war.

Kline's twenty-four books run from a title like The Quarrel to a title like Achilles and Priam, and that span tells you the shape of the thing. It opens with a priest asking for his daughter back and being refused, and a plague arriving as the answer. It closes with an old king asking for his son's corpse back, and being granted it. Everything famous that sits outside those two requests — the judgement of Paris, the horse, the sack of the city, the death of Achilles himself — belongs to other poems and later traditions. Homer chose the narrow middle, and trusted you to know the rest.

02The quarrel

Honour is not a feeling here. It is a receipt.

Agamemnon, forced to give up a captive woman, takes Achilles' captive Briseis to replace her. Modern readers reach for jealousy, but that misses the machinery. In this world a prize taken from the spoils is the public accounting of what a fighter is worth — visible, countable, and awarded in front of the whole army. To remove it is not to hurt someone's feelings; it is to publish a revised valuation of them. Achilles withdraws, and because the poem is honest about consequences, the Greeks immediately start losing. His grievance is legitimate and his response is catastrophic, and the poem declines to resolve that tension for you.

03The gods

Deathless, partisan, and strangely petty.

Anyone expecting solemn deities is in for a surprise. Homer's gods take sides, cheat, sulk, deceive each other, and get personally involved in the fighting — one book has a mortal wounding two of them. Hera drugs her husband's attention away from the battlefield so the Greeks can gain ground. Zeus spends much of the poem managing his relatives. What looks like comedy is doing serious work: the gods can afford all of it because nothing they lose is permanent. They are the control group. Every scene of divine bickering makes the human scenes heavier, because the humans are the ones who stay dead.

04Hector and Andromache

The tenderest scene in the poem is given to the enemy.

In Book VI, Hector comes off the wall to see his wife and infant son. The baby is frightened by the horsehair crest on his father's helmet, so Hector laughs, takes the helmet off, and holds him. Andromache asks him not to go back out. He knows — and says — that Troy will fall, that she will be dragged away to weave for another woman, and he goes back out anyway, because he cannot bear to be seen hanging back. This is the structural masterstroke of the whole poem. Homer was singing to Greeks, and he handed the most humane scene he wrote to the man their hero kills.

Everyone in this poem is trapped by what they believe honour requires of them — and the poem keeps showing you the bill.

05Patroclus

He gets exactly what he demanded, and it ruins him.

With the Greeks collapsing, Achilles still will not fight, but lets his closest companion Patroclus borrow his armour to frighten the Trojans back. Patroclus pushes too far and Hector kills him. This is the hinge. Achilles' withdrawal was meant to punish Agamemnon; it killed the person he loved most. His grief turns into something colder than the original anger — he returns to the field, kills Hector, and drags the body behind his chariot, day after day, past the point where any of it can help. The poem is precise about this: the rage that began as a defensible claim ends as a man abusing a corpse.

06Book XXIV

An old man kisses the hands that killed his son.

Priam, king of Troy, crosses the lines at night, alone, into the tent of the man who has been dishonouring his son's body, and begs for it back. He tells Achilles to think of his own father, far away, waiting. And Achilles does — and the two of them weep, for different people, in the same room. Then, remarkably, they eat together. Nothing is solved. Both men know Troy will fall and Achilles will not survive the war either. But a truce is agreed so Hector can be buried, and the poem simply ends there, at a funeral, with no victory in sight. It is one of the oldest stories in Europe and it closes on the enemy's grief.

One idea to keep

Grief is the only thing that crosses the line.

Every institution in the poem fails to end the quarrel: rank cannot, oaths cannot, embassies with gifts cannot, and neither can the gods. What finally moves Achilles is an old man making him think about his own father. Three thousand years later that is still the most reliable thing we have — not winning the argument, but making the other person visible as somebody's son. If you read the Iliad for one thing, read it for how long that takes, and what it costs to get there.
