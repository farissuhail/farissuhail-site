Many translators, one poet, and an editor with a view.

Kabir Helminski has been translating Rumi for more than forty years and directs the Threshold Society with Camille Helminski, a non-profit rooted in the Mevlevi tradition that Rumi’s followers founded. This anthology gathers work from many hands — Robert Bly, Coleman Barks, Camille Helminski, Andrew Harvey and others — into a single arranged introduction to the poet’s life and thought.

Reading a poet through twelve translators is not twelve views of one man. It is twelve men, and the editor decides which one you meet first.

01What an anthology is for

The corpus is enormous. You need a door.

Rumi’s output is not a book, it is a library: the Mathnawi alone runs to tens of thousands of lines across six volumes, with a vast collection of lyric verse beside it and prose discourses beyond that. Nobody begins there. An anthology exists to cut a path, and the value of a good one is entirely in the cutting — which themes are named, in what order, and what a reader is given to hold before being handed something difficult. Helminski arranges the material thematically, so the book reads as an introduction to a way of seeing rather than as a sampler of famous lines.

02The editor

Assembled by a teacher, not by a literary critic.

Helminski comes to the material as a practitioner within the Mevlevi tradition rather than as an academic editor, and that shows in every choice. The arrangement is pedagogical: it moves the way instruction moves, from the condition of the ordinary self toward what the tradition regards as its transformation, rather than following chronology or literary merit. Whether you want that depends on what you came for. It gives the collection a coherence most anthologies lack — and it means you are reading one tradition’s reading of Rumi, which is worth knowing while you read it.

03Many hands

Every translator is a second author.

The roster is the collection’s greatest strength and its central complication. Bly brings the cadence of an American poet; Camille Helminski works close to the Persian; Harvey writes with an ecstatic register of his own. Reading them side by side is genuinely instructive, because the same underlying material arrives in radically different temperatures and you can watch how much of your impression of a poet is actually an impression of his translator. The cost is that the voice you come away with is a composite that never existed, assembled from a dozen sensibilities across a century of English.

04The Barks question

Beautiful English that is not a translation.

Coleman Barks is the reason most English readers have heard of Rumi at all, and his versions are the most widely quoted poetry of their kind in modern publishing. He also does not read Persian. His method was to take existing scholarly translations and rework them into free verse, which produced something genuinely alive in English and something that is, strictly, a new poem. Two conclusions follow, and holding both is the mature position: the popularity is not fraudulent, and a reader quoting those lines as Rumi’s words is making a claim the text cannot support.

The most quoted Rumi in English is a beautiful poem by somebody else, and almost nobody has been told.

05How to read it

Not front to back. A few pages at a time.

Poetry of this kind resists being consumed at the pace of prose, and an anthology read straight through flattens into a single mood by the second section. The arrangement rewards returning: a few pages, read slowly, with the theme headings treated as the editor’s questions rather than as labels. Keeping a note of which translator produced a passage that stayed with you is worth the small effort, because it tells you where to go next — the anthology’s real function is to send you somewhere specific afterwards.

06Where to be careful

A gateway, and it should be treated as one.

Two honest notes to close on. First, the same caution that belongs with any Sufi material applies here: tasawwuf and the practices of particular orders have been debated within Muslim scholarship for centuries, an anthology of poetry settles none of that, and readers should carry questions of practice to the scholarship they trust. Second, this is an English gateway assembled with real care, not a substitute for the text. If the collection does its job, it leaves you wanting the Mathnawi itself, in a translation that tells you where every verse came from — which is the case Emily Jane O’Dell’s The Gift of Rumi makes at length.

One idea to keep

Read the editor as carefully as the poet.

Every anthology you will ever pick up has already answered the important questions before you opened it: what was worth including, what order it goes in, and which version of a person you are being introduced to. Noticing that is not cynicism. It is the difference between reading a tradition and being handed one.
