Book summary · The Mdrn Urban

For a century, Malaya was governed twice. One of the governments wasn't the British.

Wilfred Blythe spent his career in the Malayan Civil Service dealing with the Chinese secret societies at close range, then wrote the standard study of them, running from the first Chinese settlements in the Straits down to 1965. His real subject is not crime so much as authority. Over an immigrant community the colonial state could barely speak to, ritual brotherhoods supplied protection, work, arbitration, kinship and punishment, which is to say they supplied government; and the story of Malaya's secret societies is the story of a state slowly learning that it could not legislate away a service it was not yet providing.

“A body politic in miniature.” Wilfred Blythe

Where the state cannot reach its people, somebody else governs them, and charges for it.

01The China background

They began as mutual aid, and drifted into extortion.

Ritualistic societies had existed in China for centuries, built on a simple combination of self-protection and spiritual satisfaction, and drawing their imagery from popular Taoism, Buddhism and the sworn-brotherhood tradition. They also deteriorated with equal regularity: easy money corrupted them, and the shelter they offered honest traders was extended, for a fee, to bullies and extortioners. The one that mattered most for Malaya was the Triad Brotherhood, whose ritual carried a political charge, the restoration of a fallen Ming dynasty, and whose members could claim an aura of chivalry as the poor man's protector even while battening on him.

02Why they took root

A new country made the brotherhood a social necessity.

Arriving in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, the immigrant had no family, no village, no clan and no state to fall back on, so the society replaced all of them at once. Membership might be organised around dialect, home district, trade or employer, and sometimes around nothing but the fact of having landed; the initiation oath supplied the blood bond that a shared surname would have supplied at home. Blythe's point is that this was not a fringe underworld but the ordinary social architecture of a frontier population, and that for a man arriving alone into a country whose language, religion and customs were entirely foreign, the pressure to join was close to irresistible: in many cases, being engaged to labour carried membership with it.

03The parallel government

Powers that belong to a state, held by private brotherhoods.

Once the societies dominated the immigrant community, they wielded powers that in British practice belonged to the Government alone, including the death penalty, and their members treated that authority as superior to the state's. This intermediate layer of extra-legal jurisdiction quietly cancelled a good deal of official policy: well-intentioned law could be nullified before it ever reached the people it was written for. There was no central command over the whole system, however, which is why the societies spent as much energy fighting each other as defying the Government, and why commercial rivalry between them so easily became a riot.

01

What they gave

Protection, work, credit, arbitration of disputes, burial, kinship, and a place in a body politic that actually recognised you.

02

What they took

Levies on trade and labour, obedience under blood-oath, and the standing right to punish, extort, and settle rivalries in the street.

04Why policing failed

The evidence existed. Nobody could be made to give it.

Assault, extortion, murder and public affray were straightforward crimes; securing convictions for them was not. A complainant had to bring his case to a court his own society could reach, so witnesses withdrew, statements evaporated, and the man who had been robbed was often the same man who dared not name the robber. The colonial police could not read the community either: few officers spoke even a fraction of the immigrants' dialects, the Chinese constables who did were frequently society members themselves, and so were many of the interpreters everyone else depended on. A state that cannot gather evidence in a language it does not speak is not, in any practical sense, present.

05Control, registration, suppression

A century of law arriving one riot late.

The pattern repeats with painful regularity: an outbreak of society violence, a commission of enquiry, a bold Bill, then a watered-down Ordinance that manages the problem instead of solving it. The Penang riots of 1867 produced a Bill aimed at suppressing dangerous societies outright, which emerged from council as a scheme for registration. Only with the Societies Ordinance of 1889 did policy move to real suppression, and banishment became the sharpest instrument the Government held, because a society leader who was not a British subject could be removed without the evidence a court would have demanded. Blythe is clear-eyed about the cost of that shortcut, and equally clear that legislation on its own never once broke a society.

“...in which they can openly and legally play the hero and vent their grievances.”

Mao Tse-tung, 1927, on why the secret societies emptied into the peasant associations
06The Chinese Protectorate

What finally worked was a rival institution, not a harder law.

Alongside the repressive machinery, the Government extended the Chinese Protectorate across Malaya, and this is the quiet hinge of the whole book. The Protectorate gave the poor man a tribunal of his own: an official trained in his language and customs who would hear accusations, adjudicate claims, arbitrate disputes and reconcile quarrels, without court fees or lawyers. It removed at a stroke most of the grounds on which the societies justified their existence, because the service they had been selling was now available honestly and free. A rough equilibrium followed, and it is worth naming precisely what produced it: not suppression on its own, but suppression paired with a substitute.

07Equilibrium is not a cure

Driven underground, the ritual rotted and the racket remained.

The societies never disappeared; they relaxed or coiled tighter according to how hard they were pressed, and the authorities lived permanently with an unwelcome choice. Press hard, and the organisations went dark: harder to see, harder to reach, but quieter in the street. Press lightly, and they surfaced into open inter-society warfare. Underground, the old ritual thinned and the discipline it carried went with it, so ceremonial brotherhoods degenerated into hooligan gangs that kept the extortion, the protection rackets and the reprisals while discarding the code that had once dignified them. That, more than any Ming restoration, is what the secret societies became in the towns.

08War, liberation, relapse

1945 removed the controls, and the societies came straight back.

The Japanese occupation was ruthless without being effective: hundreds of society members were executed, others fled to the jungle with the resistance groups, and some served the occupiers as informers, yet the societies came through the war intact. Liberation then handed them their best years. The postwar policy of freedom of association, and the abandonment in practice of the Societies Ordinance and the banishment power, produced a Triad boom: thousands were recruited and initiated with the full ceremony, and in Penang a whole section of the community, Blythe writes, from millionaire to beggar, was subject to society taxation. It took years to restore the law, by which time the Emergency had arrived and the Government was reaching for detention powers written against the Communists that it found itself using on gangsters too.

09Independence and Pulau Senang

Emergency powers outlived the emergency that justified them.

Independence did not relax detention. If anything the new governments tightened it, on the argument that a young state needed more security rather than less, and because the end of British control removed the old escape valve of banishing troublemakers to China. Blythe records the unease this caused, and the honourable attempt to answer it: Singapore's Pulau Senang, an open rehabilitation settlement with no walls, where detainees built the camp they lived in and hundreds were released back into work. On 12 July 1963 a body of detainees burned it down and killed the Superintendent and three of his staff. The experiment was abandoned, and the argument returned to exactly where it had started, with the state holding men it could not convict.

“It is essentially an ethical problem.”

Wilfred Blythe
10Blythe's answer

Not more powers. A country worth belonging to.

Closing the study, Blythe refuses to treat detention as a solution. Holding hundreds of gangsters clears the streets and is welcomed, he concedes, but nobody should mistake it for a cure, and rehabilitation schemes founder because they reach too few men too late. His own prescription is slower and more demanding: cut off the inflow of new recruits, build local schooling and jobs for a community now largely Malayan-born rather than China-born, and let local symbols, loyalties and heroes grow strong enough to replace the imported ones the Triad tradition supplied. He saw that generational shift already under way, and thought the task of government was to help it along rather than merely to police its failures.

One idea to keep

Ask what the racket is selling, then sell it better.

Every parallel authority, from a street gang to a cartel to a patronage network, survives because it delivers something people genuinely need and cannot get elsewhere: safety, work, a hearing, a place to belong. Blythe's century of Malayan evidence says the law can raise the price of that trade but never end it, and that the only thing which ever really displaced the societies was an institution offering the same goods honestly, in the community's own language, at no cost.
